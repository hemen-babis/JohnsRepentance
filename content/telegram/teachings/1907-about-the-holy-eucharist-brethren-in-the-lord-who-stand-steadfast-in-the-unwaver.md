---About the Holy #Eucharist 

Brethren in the Lord, who stand steadfast in the unwavering Orthodox faith, grace unto you, and peace, from God our Father, and the Lord Jesus Christ. Today we will be learning about the holy Eucharist, which stands as the pinnacle of our redemption and the very lifeblood of the Church.

✍ The Incarnation: The Prologue to the Eucharist

Before we venture into the holy of holies, let us lay a foundation by reflecting upon the Incarnation, which is the cornerstone of all Christian doctrine. In the fullness of time, God, in His inscrutable wisdom, willed to be united with man. "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). This is not a mere visitation but an abiding union. The Godhead was pleased to dwell bodily (Colossians 2:9), and in this union, the natures of divinity and humanity are neither confounded nor separated but are united without mixture or change.

In this mystical union, Christ fully assumed our nature, bearing our griefs, carrying our sorrows, yet without sin. As Saint Cyril rightly proclaimed, the nature that needed salvation was not passed over. Thus, the unblemished Lamb, perfect God and perfect Man, presented Himself as the only sacrifice capable of redeeming our fallen state.

✍ The Eucharist: The Continuation of Incarnation

The Eucharist, dear faithful, is not separate from this grand narrative; it is the perpetuation of the Incarnation. Just as the divine nature did not obliterate the human in Christ, so the essence of bread and wine is not destroyed when consecrated as the Body and Blood. Here, the Church in her wisdom teaches us that in this Holy Sacrament, there is a mystical but real transformation, akin to the Incarnation itself.

As the Lord Jesus, at His Last Supper, did not speak in parables when He said, "This is my body... This is my blood" (Matthew 26:26-28), we take Him at His divine word, acknowledging a mystery that cannot be fathomed by the human mind but is apprehended by faith.

✍ The Eucharist: The Apex of Theology

In the Eucharist, we find the sum and summit of all theological truth. It is the tangible expression of God's love, the assurance of His promise, the fulfillment of the covenant. The Eucharist stands as a foretaste of the eschatological feast, where the Church militant and the Church triumphant are united in the worship of the Lamb.

This Sacrament is not a mere remembrance but an anamnesis, a making present of the one sacrifice of Calvary. In the Divine Liturgy, we are lifted above time and space, and the events of our redemption are made present to us, that we might partake of the fruits of the Tree of Life which is Christ Himself.

✍ The Eucharist: The Medicine of Immortality

Saint Ignatius of Antioch called the Eucharist the "medicine of immortality," and rightly so. For as much as the body requires bread for sustenance, so too does the soul require the Body of Christ for eternal life. In this Holy Communion, we are united to Christ and through Him to one another, forming one body, of which He is the head.

To partake of the Eucharist unworthily is to eat and drink judgment upon oneself (1 Corinthians 11:29). Hence, the Church, as a loving mother, calls her children to approach with a pure heart, having confessed and repented, that they might be made partakers of divine nature (2 Peter 1:4).

About #free_will 

Consider, my beloved, the Scriptures that recount the creation of man. "And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness" (Genesis 1:26). In these words lies a mystery profound: that man was fashioned as a mirror of God’s own liberty, bestowed with the ability to choose—a faculty divine in its root. Man does not live by constraint, but by freedom, and this is the very essence of his glory, surpassing all other creatures.

Yet, with the subtlety of a serpent, the question slithers into the garden of our mind: What is this free will? It is, I tell you, the power to incline oneself towards the good or to descent towards evil. But mark this well: though the choice is ours, the capacity to choose the good and the very notion of good comes from God. For, as the Apostle James teaches, "Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above" (James 1:17).

Now, some do err, thinking death and sin as natural to man as his very flesh. But this is not so. For sin is not the coin of our realm, nor death our native country. These are but intruders, unnatural guests that have darkened our doorstep through the misuse of our sacred gift. We were, as the holy Apostle Paul declares, predestined for life and salvation. But predestination without free will is like a painter without a canvas—it cannot manifest.

Oh, the deep wisdom of Saint Athanasius! For he did perceive the ignoble fall of man who, by his will, descends to the depths, ensnared by his own choices, and thus is ruled by passions as by cruel taskmasters. Consider then the gravity of sin: it is a voluntary servitude, a bondage self-imposed, and therein lies its tragedy.

And what of the words of the wise Solomon? They resound as a warning to our souls: we must not court death through our misdeeds or usher in ruin by our actions. For in so doing, we yield to the basest element of ourselves and deny our God-given nature.

Consider further, brethren, the angels, those ministering spirits, who likewise bear the responsibility for their actions. For freedom is not the sole province of humanity; it is shared with the heavenly hosts. And in freedom, there is accountability. Only a free agent can be subject to judgment, for only he can truly choose.

Here, too, must we ponder the harsh truth that our will can become enslaved—enslaved by desire which, when it has conceived, brings forth sin, and sin, when it is finished, brings forth death, as Saint James the Just teaches. Thus, the will itself can become the very source of death.

Yet, do not despair, for the devil indeed plays upon our will but cannot coerce it. We may be tempted, yet by our free will, we can resist. By saying "no" to sin, we assert the divine image within us.

In conclusion, though the struggle between the carnal and the spiritual is fierce, there is hope. If we, by our free will, submit to God’s will and walk in the path of righteousness, let us be steadfast in prayer and fasting. For the Lord has promised: "Ask, and it shall be given you" (Matthew 7:7). And in asking for divine aid, we do not abdicate our freedom but perfect it, aligning our will with the divine will, which is our true liberation.

Thus, may the Almighty God, who in His infinite wisdom has granted us this precious gift of free will, cut asunder the cords of sin that bind us, that we may freely choose the good and in so doing, find our rest in Him. Amen.

May the name of the Holy God be praised forever and ever in every language that He has created, today and always.

May the blessings of the Holy Mother of God, the Virgin, and the Cross be upon us. Amen.
