---Question፦ Is conditional #forgiveness biblically acceptable?  

👉 Answer፦ Peace be upon you, my child.  Your question delves into the very heart of Christian life – forgiveness, a virtue often misunderstood and challenging to practice, particularly in the face of deep hurt, like infidelity.  You ask about the balance between forgiveness and consequences, a tension that has wrestled with the faithful for centuries.

The Lord's Prayer itself instructs us to ask God to "forgive us our trespasses as we forgive those who trespass against us" (Matthew 6:12, Luke 11:4).  This suggests a reciprocity, a mirroring of divine forgiveness.  Just as God forgives us, not by eliminating the consequences of sin (sickness, death entered the world through sin), but by offering redemption and healing, so too should we forgive.

Christ's parable of the unforgiving servant (Matthew 18:23-35) powerfully illustrates this.  The king forgave an immense debt, yet the servant refused to forgive a much smaller debt owed to him.  This highlights the disproportionate mercy we receive from God and the expectation that we extend similar mercy to others.  The parable does not, however, negate the justice of the king's final punishment.  The unforgiving servant faced consequences for his hard-heartedness.

Forgiveness, in the true Christian sense, is not condoning the sin. It is not forgetting or pretending it didn't happen.  Rather, it is releasing the grip of anger and resentment, a conscious decision to let go of the desire for revenge or retribution.  This is for our own spiritual healing, as much as for the other person.  St. John Chrysostom, in his homilies on Matthew, speaks eloquently about how harboring anger harms us more than the person who wronged us.

Regarding consequences, these are often necessary for growth and repentance.  In the case of infidelity, requesting therapy, setting boundaries, or even choosing separation can be acts of love – both for oneself and the offending spouse.  These actions are not antithetical to forgiveness; they can be part of the healing process.  Think of the Prodigal Son (Luke 15:11-32). The father embraced his returning son with boundless love (forgiveness), but the son still experienced the consequences of his actions – the loss of his inheritance, the hardship he endured.  This experience ultimately led to his repentance and restoration.

Conditional forgiveness, in the sense of withholding inner forgiveness until certain conditions are met, can be a slippery slope.  It risks becoming a tool of manipulation.  However, setting boundaries and expectations for reconciliation is not inherently wrong. It's an act of wisdom, reflecting a commitment to the health and integrity of the relationship.  Just as a physician prescribes medicine for healing, so too can we encourage steps towards repentance and restoration.

Therefore, my child, strive for genuine forgiveness – releasing the bitterness and anger.  Simultaneously, recognize the wisdom in setting boundaries and seeking appropriate help, such as counseling from a priest or therapist. These are not mutually exclusive; they can work together to bring healing and, God willing, reconciliation.  

Remember the words of St. Isaac the Syrian, "He who has not tasted the sweetness of forgiveness has not tasted the goodness of God."  Pray for the strength to forgive, and for wisdom to discern the path forward.  I will continue to pray for you as well.  Please do not hesitate to return and share your progress.  May the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you.
