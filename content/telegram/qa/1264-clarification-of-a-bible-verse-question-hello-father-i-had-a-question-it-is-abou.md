#Clarification of a #Bible #Verse

👉 Question፦ Hello Father, I had a question. It is about the verse of the bible that says "love your enemy". How can we love people who are trying to destroy us? 

Answer፦ Indeed, the question you pose is of great significance, dear member. "Love your enemy," a principle taught by Christ, is often difficult to comprehend, especially when our enemies appear to bring us harm or destruction. The tenet seemingly contradicts our natural instincts of self-preservation and justice. As followers of the Orthodox faith, how then do we interpret this seemingly paradoxical instruction?

The Gospel according to Matthew, chapter 5, verse 44 tells us, "But I say to you, love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who spitefully use you and persecute you." But who exactly are these enemies that we are called to love? Surely, it is not a call to love those who aim to obliterate our faith, subvert our rights, and bring calamity upon us.

In our Orthodox belief, our true enemy is not of flesh and blood, but the devil himself. The enemy that Christ instructs us to love is not one who seeks to eradicate our faith, usurp our lands, or eradicate our presence on earth. No, it is not a call for passive submission to destructive forces.

Instead, the enemies referred to here are those among our own brothers and sisters in faith with whom we might have disputes or disagreements, those who have perhaps hurt us or wronged us in personal or communal contexts. It is these individuals, who are nevertheless our brethren in faith, that we are instructed to love and forgive.

But when it comes to those who threaten our existence, our faith, and our lands, the Bible offers other wisdom. In these cases, we are called upon to stand firm, to defend our faith and our people. The Biblical figures such as the Prophet David, who defeated Goliath, and the Prophet Moses, who stood against the Pharaoh's tyranny, exemplify this righteous resistance. It is not sinful to stand against destructive forces, indeed it is a calling of duty to protect and preserve the sanctity of life, faith, and community.

So when we interpret the command to "love your enemies," it is essential to discern the context. It means to love and forgive those who share our faith and our common humanity, even when they wrong us in minor ways. It does not imply love for those who aim to destroy our religion, our church, our country, or our lives.

Our duty extends to correction too. If a member of our Orthodox faith engages in wrongful acts, it is our responsibility to gently and lovingly correct them, making them aware of their transgressions. To oppose an evil act is not hatred but a form of love, for it aims to help the individual correct their path.

Moreover, we ought to appreciate that we are called to love even those who do not share our faith, yet are united with us in patriotism, in common goodwill for humanity, and in the fight against destructive forces. Yet, we must not tolerate those who wish to disrupt peace, alter our faith, or destruct our nation. Our forefathers have demonstrated this through their actions.

For instance, during the Adwa war, our ancestors did not stand idle in the face of forces aiming to change our religion and destroy our nation. They did not misconstrue "love your enemy" to mean passive acceptance of such atrocities. Instead, they acted righteously, defended their faith and their land, and handed down to us a heritage of steadfastness and spiritual courage.

In the same way, may God grant us wisdom to discern between our enemies and those merely disguised as such. May He give us strength to love those whom we are called to love and the courage to stand against those who threaten to disrupt our faith and peace. May we walk in His ways, loving when we must love, and resisting when we must resist, until we reach our ultimate salvation.

May God bless us all.
