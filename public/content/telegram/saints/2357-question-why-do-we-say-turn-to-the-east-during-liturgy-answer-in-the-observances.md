Question፦ Why do we say "turn to the east" during #Liturgy? 

Answer፦ In the observances of our Orthodox liturgy, the act of turning towards the east transcends mere ritualistic formality, embodying instead a profound theological and spiritual orientation that unites us, in both memory and hope, to the very essence of our faith. Let us delve deeply into this sacred tradition, that its significance might illuminate our hearts as the rising sun illuminates the world.

From time immemorial, the east has beckoned to the human spirit as the cradle of divine revelation and the harbinger of the new dawn. It is towards this sacred horizon that our churches are meticulously oriented, guiding our collective gaze towards the birthplace of salvation, where the narrative of redemption unfolds in the radiant light of God’s mercy.

The east, as the domicile of our primordial heritage, harks back to the very beginnings of mankind in the Garden of Eden, positioned in the east, according to ancient texts. This direction thus becomes a symbol of our origins and our ultimate destination, the heavenly realm promised to us. Each orientation towards the east during liturgy is a vivid remembrance of this promise, a gesture that embodies our yearning for re-entry into that lost paradise, now transformed through Christ’s salvific work into the hope of eternal life.

Moreover, the nativity of our Lord in the east serves as a testament to the dawn of God's mercy upon humanity. The Magi, guided by the celestial star, embarked on their eastward journey, a journey that each Christian symbolically undertakes during the liturgy. This star, emblematic of divine guidance, beckons us to follow in their footsteps, leading us to the manger where light eternal was born into the world. Thus, turning to the east is to remember the Incarnation, a pivotal moment when the Divine mingled with the human, illuminating our path with the promise of redemption.

The Virgin Mary, revered as the dawn heralding the Sun of Righteousness, embodies the east’s spiritual significance. Just as the sun dispels the darkness at dawn, so did the birth of Christ through Mary illuminate a world ensnared in sin's shadow. This symbolic association underscores her role in the divine economy of salvation, making our eastward turn a commemoration of her who is theotokos, the Mother of God, and the luminous portal through which the true Light came into the world.

The redemptive sacrifice of Christ, enacted in the east, further consecrates this direction in the Christian liturgical tradition. Jerusalem, the city of His passion, death, and resurrection, anchors our faith's historical and spiritual reality in the eastern landscape. In turning east, we thus commemorate the paschal mystery, the crux of our faith, where through His Cross and Resurrection, Christ triumphed over death, offering us the path to salvation.

Our ecclesiastical heritage, rooted in the eastern origins of Christianity, finds its expression in this liturgical orientation. The early Church, burgeoning forth from Jerusalem, disseminated the Gospel from this eastern heartland. In facing east, we thus align ourselves with the apostolic journey, tracing the Church’s growth from its nascent beginnings to its global expanse, a testament to the enduring and universal message of hope and salvation.

The liturgical invocation to "look to the east" is, therefore, a call to remembrance, to faith, and to hope. It is an invitation to contemplate the manifold ways in which God’s grace has been manifested from the east - through creation, through the Incarnation, through the saints and martyrs who have gone before us, and in the eschatological hope of Christ’s Second Coming from the east.

In this sacred act, we are not merely turning our bodies towards a geographical direction; we are orienting our souls towards the Light of the World, whose rising we eagerly await.
