2.3. The Season of Tsedey

Next comes Tsedey, a season of transition, stretching from Megabit 26 to Sene 25. It includes the months of Miyaziya, Ginbot, and Sene.

Tsedey contains the qualities of the other seasons. On one hand, it is the time of the Belig harvest—sown, reaped, and threshed. On the other, it is the time when the land is prepared for the coming Keremt rains. Thus, it is both the season of conclusion and of preparation.

Saint Yared in his hymn declares."ይሁብ ዝናመ ተወን"(yihub zname tewen)  the Belg rains are given.” The people labor diligently, sowing where there is fall rain, and where there is none, plowing and preparing the fields. An old proverb says, “One Sene does not replace two Sene that are harvested,” reminding us that diligence today cannot be postponed to tomorrow.

2.4 The Season of Keremt

Keremt, from Sene 26 to Meskerem 25, is the season of rain and water. Its months are Hamle, Nehase, Pagume, and Meskerem. Water, in this season, pours forth from heaven, washing the earth, quenching its fire, and blessing it with moisture.

Saint Yared declares:The Lord of the Ages, the Almighty, brings us winter every year, and the clouds hear His word.” The rains of Keremt are not mere waters, but the mercy and blessing of God poured upon His people. It is the season of renewal and life-giving abundance, when the earth, like a servant, listens to the word of its Master.

3. The Year of the Evangelists

The years of John, Matthew, and Mark are 365 days and 6 hours (from Meskerem 1 to Pagume 5).

The year of Luke alone is 366 days (from Meskerem 1 to Pagume 6).

Thus, the four Evangelists themselves mark the rhythm of the year, binding time to the Gospel.


4. The Months of the Year

The word for month in Geʿez is Werh, which in Amharic is Werh and in plural form Wereh. In the Ethiopian calendar, there are twelve months of thirty days each. At the end, five days are added or six in the leap year of Luke. This differs from the Western system, where months vary in length.

Unlike the European calendar, the months are equal in length, reflecting order and balance.

Conclusion

Thus, beloved, the Ethiopian reckoning of time is no mere human invention but a reflection of divine wisdom. It marks the turning of the seasons with prayer, labor, and thanksgiving. Each season carries its own mystery: Keremt brings rain, Metsew brings flowers, Bega brings harvest and joy, Tsedey brings diligence and preparation.

As Saint Yared and the Scriptures testify, God Himself ordains the times and the seasons, that man may remember His power and give thanks. Truly, time itself becomes a hymn to the Creator: the rains, the sun, the harvest, and the plow all sing the praises of the Lord who gives seed to the sower and bread to the eater.

May the name of the Holy God be praised forever and ever in every tongue that He has created, today and always. 

May the blessings of God's mother, the virgin, and the cross be upon us.
